# Luna Reyes Walks Through Stop Finder

A poet and flâneur notices doors. Buildings number them in a thousand ways, and most drivers—most _people_—never think to ask why. This course teaches the why. It teaches with 3D geometry and click-and-answer quizzes, not lectures. I walked through all five parts, and here is what I saw.

## The Arrival: Part 1's Generosity

The first building is a small 2-story four-plex, units 101–108, red and beige, sitting on flat olive ground under flat blue sky. Yellow arrows point downward—_read this way_—and the prompt says: memorize the pattern. There is no rushing. The prompt banner, dark and centered, speaks in amber caps: **yellow arrows**, **memorize**, **fill in the missing unit numbers**. The words are kind, not harsh. And below, the green pill button says Continue.

I like this beginning. A learner arrives into a calm space. The camera holds steady. The building is simple enough to hold in the mind. The arrows are bold and unambiguous. The sky does not distract.

But—and here is the first small friction—the prompt is dense. There are five emphases in one paragraph. A driver in a hurry, or a new reader, might lose the thread between "count up in order" and "click Continue." The amber color is good; the bold is good; but perhaps _one_ key phrase per sentence would breathe better.

## The Pattern Emerges: Part 2's Verticality

Now: a four-story walk-up. Units 128–130, 228–230, 328–330, 428–430. A massive yellow arrow points skyward, running the whole height of the building. Floor badges sit on the left: `1`, `2`, `3`, `4`. The logic is revealed: first digit = floor.

This is where the course becomes a teacher. The visual is not just a model; it is an _argument_. The arrow says "up." The badges reinforce it. A learner's eye is guided. The 3D scene has rotated to show the facade more directly, and it works—the units read in vertical columns, as they should.

The quiz panel, when opened, floats on the right in a dark container with four options (a, b, c, d). The type is large and readable. Correct answers are confirmed; I assume wrong ones shake or redden, though I don't see that in the screenshots. The interactivity is clean. Clickable doors are the only doors that matter; the rest remain geometry.

## The Complexity Arrives: Part 3's Banner

Part 3 widens the problem. A 2-story row, five doors per floor, units ranging into the thousands: 1101–1110, 1201–1210. A grey banner hangs over the roof: **Building 1**. The prompt now asks the learner not just to fill numbers but to _name the building_.

This is a shift in cognitive load. The learner must:
1. Decode the numbering pattern (leading digits = building).
2. Infer the building name from the units shown.
3. Perform both tasks in sequence.

The banner solves a real visual problem—at this scale, with many doors, the building's identity is easy to lose. The banner anchors it. But I wonder: is it clear to a new learner that they are being asked to _choose_ a building name from options? The prompt does not show those options; I assume they appear after clicking Continue. This is a moment where the interface might benefit from a tiny preview or hint.

## The Demands Mount: Part 4's Spin

Building 10 is a square block. Units wrap around the front AND back: 10101–10109 visible, and presumably more on the reverse. The prompt is explicit: **drag to spin around**. Study both faces before answering.

Here the 3D scene becomes a _tool_, not a decoration. The learner must rotate the model, read door numbers from an unfamiliar angle, then click a blank and guess. This is the closest the course comes to a real-world task—a driver standing in front of a building, turning to read addresses on the back.

The camera is in orbit-control mode. The model does not snap back to its default angle after a drag. This is good; it trusts the learner. But—and I say this gently—the prompt's instruction to "drag to spin" might be missed by someone used to clicking, not dragging. A subtle visual cue on the model (like a hand cursor, or a faint grid showing rotation) could help. The course is patient with text; could it be patient with a visual hint?

## The Finale: Part 5's Walk-Around

Building 6 stands at the corner, a three-story structure numbered 601–620 around all four sides. No floor digit; just a running count. A new UI element appears: four buttons on the left, labeled Front / Back / Left / Right. Clicking each button rotates the camera to face that side, showing the door numbers arranged vertically.

This is the moment I most admired. The buttons are a meta-solution to a real problem: "I need to see all four sides, but I can't rotate smoothly." They are not flashy, but they are _kind_. They say: "If you're lost, snap to a cardinal direction." A learner on a phone—or someone without the spatial reasoning for free orbit—can now succeed.

The buttons are dark, rounded, readable. They sit on the left, leaving the right side for the quiz panel. The layout is balanced. And yet: the prompt is very long. It explains five concepts (no floor digit, single count, Building 6 as example, range 601–620, lowest/highest distinction). The amber bolding helps, but the density remains. A learner might scroll or ignore; a few key facts upfront, with the rest unfolding through interaction, might serve better.

## Colors and Mood

The palette is consistent across all five parts:
- **Sky:** pale blue, calming, never a distraction.
- **Ground:** olive green, muted, stable.
- **Buildings:** red, beige, tan—earthy colors that feel residential.
- **Doors:** dark navy rectangles; clearly not doors, but clearly clickable.
- **Plates:** white labels with navy text, high contrast, legible.
- **UI:** dark translucent grey for prompts and panels, pale green for buttons.
- **Accents:** amber for emphasis in prompts, yellow for arrows.

The color system is restrained and intentional. It does not overwhelm. A learner's eye finds what matters: the building, the numbers, the call to action. The palette would work equally in light and dark ambient conditions.

## The Click and the Response

When a door is clicked, the quiz panel opens on the right. The prompt changes: "Which unit is this?" Four options appear. The door itself is now highlighted or slightly altered (I infer from the screenshots), drawing attention.

What I do not see in the screenshots: confirmation of a correct answer. I assume it fills the plate with green, perhaps with a checkmark or a satisfying animation. And a wrong answer—I assume it shakes the panel or shows red feedback. The course is static in these images, but the _feeling_ of feedback matters enormously for learning. 

**Recommendation:** If the feedback is subtle, consider making it a touch more celebratory on a correct answer. A learner who has decoded a pattern deserves to feel successful. A small confetti burst (as the briefing mentions) is perfect for completion; even a brief green flash and a chime for each correct answer would help.

## Mobile and Constraints

I saw two mobile screenshots (390×844 px). Part 1 and Part 5 on a phone.

On mobile, the UI does not vanish; it adapts. The "Jump to Part" buttons stay in the top-left corner. The prompt banner scales to fit. The quiz panel (when open) takes up less real estate, but it fits. The 3D model is touchable: pinch to zoom, drag to rotate.

What I worry about: the prompt banner is very full. On mobile, it might cover half the screen. And the quiz panel, which floats on the right on desktop, could be repositioned below the 3D scene on phone—or it could be a modal overlay. I cannot tell from the screenshots whether this is handled already, but it is worth checking.

The "Walk around" buttons in Part 5 are essential on mobile. Without them, a learner with tiny fingers might struggle to drag-rotate smoothly. The buttons provide an escape route. Good thinking.

## What Works

1. **The curriculum is visual.** A learner does not read about numbering systems; they see and decode them.
2. **Progression is gentle.** Part 1 is a 4-plex; Part 5 is a 20-door complex. The difficulty ramps steadily.
3. **The prompts are honest.** They say what will happen: "memorize," "keep the pattern going," "choose the correct building number." No false mystery.
4. **The UI is uncluttered.** Buttons are where you expect them. Colors do not jar.
5. **Interactive navigation works.** The "Jump to Part" buttons let a learner skip around; the "Walk around" buttons solve the 3D navigation problem.

## What Could Breathe Better

1. **Prompt density.** Each banner contains 2–3 ideas. Separating key instructions from context might lighten the cognitive load. Perhaps a one-line action at the top, with expandable details below.
2. **Visual hints for gestures.** "Drag to spin" is stated in text. A subtle hand cursor or a rotating arrow on the model might reinforce it without words.
3. **Mobile layout.** The prompt banner might need a collapse button or a repositioning on small screens to give the 3D scene breathing room.
4. **Feedback visibility.** I trust there is good feedback (green, red, shake, chime), but its prominence and speed matter for learner confidence. Quick, clear, celebratory.
5. **Building names.** In Part 3, the learner must choose a building name, but the options are not shown in the example view. A preview or hint—"You'll choose from options like A, B, C"—might set expectations.

## The Feeling

Walking through Stop Finder is like walking with a guide who knows buildings and wants you to see them clearly. The guide does not condescend. The guide does not rush. The scenes are rendered in low-poly geometry, which sounds spare, but it is not—it is focused. A learner looks at a door and thinks, "I need to read that number. Why is it placed there? What pattern does it follow?"

For a delivery driver, a student of urban layout, or anyone curious about the small systems that hold cities together, this is generous teaching. The course respects the learner's time and attention.

## Three Wishes

**First:** that the prompt banners could be a touch lighter—perhaps a one-line headline in amber, followed by a smaller, greyed detail. Let the learner's eye settle before receiving the full briefing.

**Second:** that the feedback (correct, incorrect, completion) be as celebratory and clear as the prompt. A learner who decodes a pattern should _feel_ that they succeeded. If a course can fire confetti at the end, it can give each small win a moment of recognition.

**Third:** that more learners—especially those navigating cities on foot, by bike, or by car—encounter this course. Understanding how buildings number themselves is a small literacy, but it changes how you move through the world. You stop looking at facades as random and start reading them as systems.

---

This course teaches reading. In an age of GPS, that might seem quaint. But reading a building's face teaches you to look at buildings at all. And that is how a city becomes legible.
