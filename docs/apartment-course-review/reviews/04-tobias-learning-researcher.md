# Review: Stop Finder Apartment Course — by Tobias Lindqvist, Educational Researcher

This course page does something I do not see often enough: it lets the learner *work with* the visual problem instead of *reading about* it. The 3D apartment buildings are not decoration. They are the curriculum. And that matters.

## What Works Well

**Scaffolding through progressive complexity.** The five parts build exactly the way a procedural skill should. You start with sequential numbering on a small 2-story 4-plex (yellow arrows make the flow explicit), then layer in floor-digit patterns, then building numbers, then front-and-back numbering, and finally a single running count with no floor logic. Each new rule is introduced with a worked example in real geometry, not in abstract text. A learner sees *where* the pattern lives on the building itself—not merely hears that "the first digit is the floor," but stands in front of units 201, 202, 203 on floor 2 and watches that principle materialize. This is how procedural memory forms.

**Worked examples with intentional fading.** Each of the five parts opens with a marked-up building (yellow arrows, floor badges, building name banner). The arrows and labels do real pedagogical work—they direct attention to the exact feature you are learning. Then the quiz stages remove these supports; a learner must now *retrieve* the pattern without the visual cues. That is retrieval practice, and it is timed well. The fade happens only after the rule has been introduced in context, not before. A delivery driver who struggles with the first quiz can go back to the worked example, and the arrows will still be there.

**Clarity in the prompts.** The top banner uses bold keywords ("floor," "building number," "running count") that name the exact concept being taught. The language is direct: *the first digit tells you the floor*. Not vague, not wrapped in procedural jargon. And crucially, each prompt tells the learner what to do next: "memorize the pattern," "drag to look around," "click Ready." The learner is never uncertain about what step comes now.

**Immediate, graded feedback.** When you click a door and choose an option, you learn whether you are right or wrong within 0.3 seconds. Right answers turn the option green and lock the plate. Wrong answers shake and turn red. There is no delay, no ambiguity, no page reload. For a procedural task, that speed is essential—it keeps the learner's mental model intact across the choice and the feedback.

**Interaction that *teaches* the building structure.** In Parts 4 and 5, the learner must drag the camera to see all four faces of a building. This is not a gimmick. It teaches the spatial fact that doors run *around* a building, not just along one wall. A delivery driver who understands the 3D envelope of the building will navigate it faster than one who only memorizes a number sequence. The interface makes that spatial reasoning inescapable.

## What Needs Rethinking

**Quiz options are too uniform in difficulty.** In the early stages, the wrong choices are generated by adding or subtracting a fixed offset (−10, −5, −3, −2, −1, +1, +2, +3, +5, +7, +10) from the correct answer. In stage 1, when the pattern is sequential 101–108, this often produces plausible-sounding alternatives (e.g., 112 vs. 114 vs. 102 vs. 122). But in later stages, especially Part 3 and beyond, some distractors become absurdly far from the correct building or floor. If the answer is 1205 (Building 1, floor 2, door 5), an option like 6103 (Building 6, floor 1, door 3) is so obviously wrong that it teaches nothing—it is not a misconception, just noise. **A better approach:** Generate wrong options that embody plausible mistakes—e.g., a correct floor but wrong door, or correct building but a door that does not exist on that floor. That way, the quiz checks whether the learner understands the structure, not whether they can eliminate nonsense.

**The "memorize the pattern" instruction in Part 1 is vague.** The prompt tells the learner to "memorize the pattern," but there is no clear retrieval test to show *how much* memorization is enough. The Continue button appears immediately after the example, and clicking it moves to quiz stage 01. But how does the learner know whether they have internalized the pattern well enough to solve a quiz? There is no intermediate checkpoint—no "try to say the next unit number without looking" or a low-stakes retrieval task on the example building itself. The transition from worked example to high-stakes quiz is abrupt. **A small fix:** Add a brief mini-quiz *on the same building* before the Continue button appears, with the worked example visible. Let the learner fail on that practice building—it has no penalty and low stakes—and then move to the next building with more confidence.

**The building-number selection in Part 3 asks for *inference* after *recall*.** In stages 8 and 9, a learner fills in missing door numbers, then must "name the building" via a banner. This is sound, but the quiz for the banner number does not happen until all doors are solved. By then, the learner has already seen the building number printed on every solved door (e.g., 7101, 7103, etc.). The "inference" is just copying; there is no real retrieval practice on that building number. **Better approach:** Ask the learner to name the building *before* filling in the door numbers. That way, they must extract the pattern from context, not read off the answer they have already constructed.

**The Walk-Around panel in Part 5 breaks spatial continuity.** The Front/Back/Left/Right buttons snap the camera to cardinal views. This is practical, but it undermines the spatial reasoning that Part 4 was teaching. In Part 4, the learner learned to *physically rotate* around a building to see all doors. In Part 5, they can now *teleport*. A learner who relies solely on the buttons never builds a mental map of the building's geometry; they just memorize which doors are on which wall. **A cleaner design:** Keep the drag-to-rotate interaction as the primary method. Use the buttons as an *optional* aid for learners who struggle with the camera, but guide learners toward spatial exploration first. Or add visual feedback (a compass rose or "you are here" indicator) that persists and updates as the camera moves, so the learner builds a coherent spatial model.

**Confetti on completion feels like an AI-generated afterthought.** When a learner finishes all 14 stages, the page fires confetti. Confetti is not bad, but it has no connection to the learning goal. A delivery driver does not care about confetti; they care about being able to find an apartment fast. **A more meaningful reward:** Show a summary—"You solved 14 buildings and learned the five numbering patterns"—and perhaps suggest a real-world application ("Next time you deliver to a new building, look for the pattern before you ring any doorbells"). That connects the skill to its purpose.

## Subtle Pedagogical Strengths

The sky is a flat, neutral light blue; the ground is olive green; there are no shadows. This is *not* photorealistic, but it is precisely the right choice for learning. A cluttered, photo-real environment would distract from the door numbers. The low-poly geometry focuses attention on the structure and the unit numbers, not the texture of the brick. The flat lighting ensures that every door plate is equally readable from every angle.

The prompt banner stays visible throughout the example stages. That is deliberate fading: the worked example still has the cognitive support (the arrow and the instruction), but once the quiz begins, the banner changes to task instructions ("Click a door marked ?") and the arrows vanish. A learner who is struggling can click Back and see the worked example again with its full scaffolding.

The quiz header changes per building ("Building 2 — keep the pattern going," vs. "Building B — Easy — the first digit is the floor"). These tiny changes in language keep the learner grounded in *why* they are choosing a number, not just *which* number to choose. It is low-stakes narrative design.

## Summary

Stop Finder's apartment course is a solid, thoughtful piece of instructional design. It respects the learner's procedural memory, it scaffolds complexity honestly, and it uses interaction and geometry to teach concepts that text cannot. The 3D visuals are not eye candy; they are load-bearing structures. The one clear miss is the uniformly generated quiz distractors, which can veer into nonsense and waste a learner's cognitive effort. A smaller refinement—adding a practice retrieval task before transitioning from worked example to quiz—would tighten the feedback loop. For a delivery driver or anyone learning apartment-numbering patterns, this page teaches a real skill and does it well.

The question I keep returning to is: Does a learner *actually* understand the spatial and numeric pattern, or do they just learn to click the right letter fast? To answer that, I would watch three delivery drivers use the page, observe what they say as they drag and click, and follow them a week later to a real building they have never seen. Do they apply the pattern, or do they ask for directions? That is the test. But on the page itself, the design choices point in the right direction.
