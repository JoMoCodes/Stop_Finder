This is a *beautiful* teaching model. If this were sitting on my workbench—scaled up and built in plaster and paint—I'd be genuinely delighted.

## What Works (and Why)

**The proportion and silhouette are bang-on.** Every building reads clearly: the 2-story 4-plex has that classic squat charm of a garden apartment; the 4-story tower (Part 2) towers appropriately without looking awkward; the wide row (Part 3) reads as a row. This is the hardest thing in miniature architecture—nailing the *feel* of a type—and you've got it.

**The color palette is warm and intentional.** Rust-red siding alternating with tan brick, that sage green ground plane, the warm ivory trim and canopy—these are colors that *love* each other. The green doors pop beautifully against the warm walls. It reads as a cohesive diorama scene, not a computer-graphics default palette. This gives the whole thing character that a learner responds to, even subconsciously.

**Tactile detail work is restrained but excellent.** The band separators between floors have weight. The window frames are crisp with proper trim (even though—nitpick—they're slightly undersized, see below). The floor-to-floor balcony railings and staircases show thoughtful geometry—they're simple but believable. This is how a good model maker thinks: every detail earns its place.

**The arrows are pedagogically *and* visually perfect.** That bright yellow 3D arrow flowing from door to door, and the floor badges on Part 2 climbing upward—these do two jobs at once. They teach the reading order. And they're gorgeous to look at; you see them and want to follow them. That's excellent design.

**The Part 5 "Walk Around" navigation is delightful.** Front / Back / Left / Right buttons that literally rotate the camera to each side of the building—this is how a designer inspects a physical model! A learner can walk around it just like you'd walk around a miniature building to see all four faces. That's smart pedagogy wearing a fun-to-use interface.

**UI layout doesn't fight the scene.** The instructions stay top-center, the jump menu hides in the top-left, the quiz panel sits on the right, the bottom buttons are tucked low. None of it obscures the building. The canvas owns the space.

**Quiz feedback is snappy.** The shake animation on a wrong answer, the green highlight on correct answers, the instant response when you click—this feels responsive, which makes learning feel fast and rewarding.

## What Needs Work (Honestly)

**The lighting is too flat.** This is the single biggest hit to the charm. A real diorama setup uses side lighting (a lamp raking across the scene) or directional light at a steep angle to create shadow play and depth. Right now you have a hemisphere light (overcast sky?) plus a sun that's high enough that it barely shadows the buildings. The result: everything feels like a computer render, not a model on a shelf.

*Why this matters to a learner:* Flat lighting makes the buildings feel like they're floating in digital space. Shadow and depth make them feel *real*, tactile, placed in a world. Even a learner who doesn't consciously notice the lighting will feel more engaged.

**The ground is abstract, not grounded.** It's a flat olive plane with a grey road strip. A good diorama has texture, curbs, a bit of weathering—something that says "this building is sitting in a real place." Right now the ground feels like a game board, not a street.

**Window glazing could read louder.** The windows are there, but they're recessed and don't catch much light. Real model makers often over-glaze windows (make them bigger or brighter) because windows are what catch light and give a model personality. Yours are a bit shy.

**Mobile responsiveness is tricky.** The mobile Part 1 quiz screenshot shows the building getting stretched tall and the quiz panel pushed down. On a phone at 390px wide, the layout breaks. You might want a horizontal mobile layout where the quiz sits below the building, or a fullscreen building with a bottom quiz overlay.

**Part 5 feels overcrowded at the isometric angle.** When viewing Building 6 from the default corner-view angle, there are 20 doors (numbers 601–620) spread across all four sides—some are small and hard to read. This isn't a *flaw* per se (it's intentionally complex), but it's the one stage where the model starts to feel busy rather than clear. When you click "Walk around" and jump to a single face (back, left, right), it becomes perfectly legible again. Smart, but worth noting.

**Staircase detail could go further.** The switchback stairs on the tower (Part 2) and the stair at right on the row (Part 3) are simplified—12 and 11 steps respectively. Real model staircases are often the proudest detail because they're geometric and hard to get right. Yours are functional but not eye-catching. (Small thing, but staircase detail is where model makers flex.)

## The Pedagogy Works Because the Visual Works

The five-part progression (sequential → floor-digit → building-digit → front-and-back → running-count) is *conceptually* solid. But what makes it *stick* is that each building *looks* like it belongs to its lesson. The learner isn't just reading instructions; they're walking around a physical model that gets progressively more complex. That's powerful.

The yellow arrows on Part 1, the big floor badges climbing on Part 2, the building name banner on Part 3—these are visual scaffolds that make the pattern obvious before you're quizzed on it. Good teaching, good model.

## Small Wishes (Polish)

- Add a contact shadow or slight edge-darkening to make the buildings sit on the ground instead of floating.
- Increase the sun's angle or add a second side light to create visible shadow across the facades—even subtle shadow adds so much depth.
- On mobile, consider a 2×2 quiz button grid below the building (landscape) or a fullscreen building with a bottom sheet quiz (portrait).
- Maybe a tiny weathered texture on the ground (bumps, a slight color variation) to make it feel less like a game board.

## Closing

This is a course that *feels* like it was made by someone who loves models and detail. The technical execution is clean, the pedagogy is sound, and the visual design has character. It's the kind of thing I'd show to another model maker and say: "Look how they solved the problem of making complex architecture legible and fun." The learner—whether a driver learning to read apartment numbers or just someone curious—will benefit from both the teaching and the *charm* of the thing.

If you wanted to take it further, lean into the physical-model aesthetic: sharper lighting, weathered textures, more dramatic shadows. Right now it's excellent digital architecture education. With those tweaks, it could *feel* like you're learning by walking around an actual miniature.
